USDA Safety Standards

Why they exist, what they cover, and where to go for the full, authoritative guidelines.

JarCheck's Safety Shield Analyzer is a helpful screening tool, not a replacement for the USDA and your local Cooperative Extension office. For any recipe you're unsure about, especially low-acid or mixed-ingredient recipes, confirm with a primary source before canning.

Why USDA Guidelines Exist

Home canning is genuinely safe when done correctly, and genuinely risky when it isn't. The biggest danger is Clostridium botulinum, a bacterium that can grow in low-acid, oxygen-free environments — exactly the conditions inside a sealed jar — and produce a toxin that isn't always detectable by taste, smell, or appearance. USDA and the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) publish tested processing times, acidity requirements, and equipment guidelines specifically to eliminate that risk.

The Core Standards

1. Acidity determines the method. High-acid foods (pH 4.6 or below — most fruits, pickles, and properly acidified tomatoes) can be safely processed in a water bath canner. Low-acid foods (pH above 4.6 — vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood) require a pressure canner, which reaches the higher temperature (240°F/116°C) needed to destroy botulism spores.

2. Recipes are not interchangeable. A tested recipe's exact ratios of acid, sugar, and density have been verified to be safe at that specific combination. Substituting ingredients, reducing sugar, adding extra vegetables, or thickening with flour or cornstarch can all change how heat penetrates the jar — even if the result looks and tastes fine.

3. Altitude changes processing time. Water boils at a lower temperature at higher elevations, so both water bath and pressure canning need to be adjusted for your altitude to reach a safe internal temperature.

4. Headspace matters. The empty space left at the top of a jar affects whether it seals properly and whether food expands safely during processing.

5. Equipment must be in good condition. Pressure canner gauges should be tested annually (many local Extension offices do this for free), and jars, lids, and bands should be inspected each season.

Consult More Than One Source

We built JarCheck to make safety screening fast and accessible, but no single tool — ours included — should be your only check, especially for a recipe you plan to store and eat months later. Before canning something you're unsure about, we'd encourage you to:

  • Cross-check with the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning
  • Search the recipe on the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) site
  • Call your local Cooperative Extension office — most offer free canning safety guidance
  • When in doubt, choose a recipe with a clearly published, tested processing time over one that doesn't have one
Primary sources worth bookmarking:
  • National Center for Home Food Preservation — nchfp.uga.edu
  • USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning — available via NCHFP
  • Your state's Cooperative Extension Service (search "[your state] extension home canning")

A Note on Signs of Spoilage

If a jar's lid is bulging, the seal has failed, the contents look cloudy in a way that wasn't there before, or you see any mold, do not taste it — discard it. Mold growth in particular can raise a food's pH, meaning a high-acid food can effectively become low-acid and unsafe, even in the parts that look unaffected.
